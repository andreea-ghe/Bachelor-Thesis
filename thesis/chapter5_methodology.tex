\chapter{Methodology: Proposed Extensions to Jigsaw}
\label{chap:ch5}

This chapter presents the methodological foundation of this thesis: three targeted extensions to the Jigsaw framework for 3D fractured object reassembly. The original Jigsaw pipeline is first summarized to establish the baseline architecture and identify the insertion points of the proposed modifications. The chapter then describes the three contributions investigated in this work: a pair geometric attention bias for fragment-level relational information, a gated double attention module for controlled feature refinement, and an ICP refinement step for improving pairwise pose estimates before global synchronization.

\section{Baseline Pipeline and Modification Points}

As introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:ch4}, Jigsaw is a hybrid learning-based framework for multi-piece 3D fracture assembly that combines deep learning with classical geometric algorithms. Its end-to-end pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:jigsaw}) consists of four modules: a front-end feature extractor (PointNet++ backbone with self-attention and cross-attention layers), a surface segmentation module that classifies points as fracture or intact, a multi-part matching module that establishes global point-to-point correspondences via primal-dual descriptors and Sinkhorn optimal transport, and a pose recovery module that estimates pairwise transformations via RANSAC and synchronizes them globally via Shonan averaging \cite{dellaert2020shonan}.

Point clouds are processed through a PointNet++ backbone with attention layers (feature extraction), classified into fracture and intact surfaces (segmentation), globally matched using primal-dual descriptors with Sinkhorn optimal transport (matching), and aligned via RANSAC and Shonan averaging (pose recovery).}

\section{Front-End Feature Extractor}
\paragraph{Baseline component.}

Jigsaw uses a PointNet++ backbone (see Section~\ref{sec:pointnet}) to extract per-point geometric features at multiple scales, followed by attention layers that model relationships both within and across fragments. PointNet++ is chosen over PointNet because its hierarchical architecture---using Farthest Point Sampling, local neighborhood grouping, and recursive PointNet applications---preserves the fine-grained local geometry of fracture surfaces, which is critical for matching complementary fragments.

While PointNet++ provides strong static embeddings for local geometric regions, fractured object reassembly also requires modeling how these features relate to one another, both within the same fragment and across different fragments. This relational context is captured through two attention layers.

First, a Point Transformer self-attention layer models the internal structure of each fragment:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:self_attn}
f_p^s = \sum_{f_q \in N(p)} \text{softmax}(\text{MLP}_s((W_Q^s f_p - W_K^s f_q + p^{enc}))) \odot (W_V^s f_q + p^{enc})
\end{equation}

The attention neighborhood \(N(p)\) is restricted to the \(k\)-nearest neighbors, keeping computation tractable while capturing local geometric structure. The mapping function \(\text{MLP}_s\) produces vector-valued attention weights rather than scalars, enabling per-channel modulation (\(\odot\)) for a more expressive representation of local 3D variations. Relative positional encodings \(p^{enc}\) are added to make the representation sensitive to the physical displacement between points.

After encoding each fragment's internal structure, a \textbf{cross-attention} layer models inter-fragment relationships. In this layer, queries are obtained from one fragment while keys and values come from other fragments, allowing local features on one fracture surface to interact with features from other fragments. This supports the discovery of point-to-point correspondences required for reassembly. A standard multi-head attention block is applied:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cross_attn}
F^c = \text{FFN}(W_O^m(\text{cat}(head_1, \dots, head_n)))
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cross_head}
head_i = \text{Attention}(W_{Q_i}^m F^s, W_{K_i}^m F^s, W_{V_i}^m F^s)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attention}
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}

Here, \(FFN(\cdot)\) consists of two linear layers with ReLU and LayerNorm. This cross-attention layer is the insertion point for the pair geometric attention bias (Section~\ref{par:pair_attn_limitations}), and the entire self-attention + cross-attention block is duplicated by the gated double attention extension.

\subsection{Proposed Improvement: Pair Geometric Attention Bias}

The standard cross-attention mechanism treats all pairs of points from different fragments in the same way, relying only on learned feature similarity. As a result, the model does not explicitly account for the spatial relationship between fragments. In practice, this means that points belonging to nearby fragments are considered just as likely as those from distant or unrelated fragments. In multi-piece reconstruction, where the number of possible matches grows rapidly, this lack of geometric awareness can make learning less efficient and more prone to errors.

To address this limitation, we draw inspiration from GPAT's "Pair Attention" module \cite{Li2023GPAT} and introduce \textbf{geometric inductive bias} into the attention mechanism. Concretely, we add a learnable scalar bias $b_{ij}$ for each pair of fragments $(i,j)$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pair_bias_attn}
\alpha_{pq} = \frac{\mathbf{q}_p^T \mathbf{k}_q}{\sqrt{d_k}} + b_{ij}.
\end{equation}
This bias depends only on the geometric relationship between fragments and is shared across all point pairs between them. In this way, the model can incorporate coarse spatial information while still relying on learned features for fine-grained matching.

\paragraph{Geometric Feature Encoding.}
The bias $b_{ij}$ is computed from simple geometric descriptors at the fragment level. First, we consider the distance between fragment centroids:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:centroid_distance}
\mathbf{p}_i = \frac{1}{N_i} \sum_{x \in P_i} x, \quad d_{ij} = \|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j\|_2.
\end{equation}
This distance is transformed using a Gaussian Radial Basis Function (RBF) encoding, which allows the model to learn flexible, non-linear relationships.

In addition, we include angular information that captures how fragments are arranged relative to each other. For this, we compute triplet angles between fragments and aggregate them into a descriptor. This provides a notion of global spatial configuration, complementing the pairwise distance.

\paragraph{Bias Integration.}
The distance and angle features are concatenated and passed through a small multi-layer perceptron:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bias_mlp}
b_{ij} = \text{MLP}([\mathbf{d}_{ij}; \mathbf{r}_{ij}]).
\end{equation}
The resulting scalar is then added to the attention logits for all point pairs between fragments $i$ and $j$, encouraging the model to favor geometrically plausible matches before applying the softmax.

\paragraph{Motivation and Expected Role.}
In contrast to methods such as GPAT, where pairwise geometric descriptors are updated as fragment poses are progressively refined, the proposed integration into Jigsaw is deliberately minimal. The bias is introduced without changing the rest of the pipeline, allowing us to evaluate whether a GPAT-inspired geometric prior can transfer to a single-pass, correspondence-based architecture. A pairwise bias provides a lightweight mechanism for modulating attention at the fragment-pair level, while still allowing the point-level features to determine the fine-grained correspondences. The purpose of this module is not to replace Jigsaw's learned matching mechanism, but to investigate whether coarse fragment-pair information can provide a useful auxiliary signal during attention.

\paragraph{Architectural Considerations.}
\label{par:pair_attn_limitations}
The bias is meaningful when the attention softmax is computed over candidate points from multiple fragments, because different target fragments can receive different fragment-pair biases. In the two-piece setting, or when attention is normalized separately for each fragment pair, the same scalar is added to all logits associated with the target fragment and therefore has little direct effect after the softmax normalization.

More fundamentally, the effectiveness of this bias depends on the coordinate frame in which the fragment-level descriptors are computed. In architectures such as GPAT \cite{Li2023GPAT}, fragment poses are iteratively updated through geometric recycling. After each refinement step, centroid distances and angular relationships are recomputed in a progressively more meaningful spatial configuration. In that setting, the pair encoder receives an increasingly informative geometric signal.

Jigsaw follows a different design philosophy. It does not iteratively update fragment poses inside the neural network; instead, it predicts point correspondences in a single forward pass and performs pose recovery afterward through RANSAC and transformation synchronization. Moreover, under the standard preprocessing protocol, fragments are independently normalized and randomly rotated before being passed to the model. As a result, absolute centroid distances and global angular relationships are less informative than in iterative pose-refinement architectures.

For this reason, the proposed pair bias should be interpreted as a lightweight compatibility experiment: it tests whether a GPAT-inspired fragment-level prior can still provide useful regularization when inserted into Jigsaw's single-pass attention pipeline. The empirical results in Section~\ref{sec:pair_attn_results} show that this transfer is limited under the current preprocessing, but the experiment remains informative because it clarifies when geometric pair attention is expected to be useful.

\subsection{Proposed Improvement: Gated Double Attention}

The original Jigsaw architecture applies a single self-attention layer followed by a cross-attention layer to extract and propagate geometric features. While this design enables both intra-fragment and inter-fragment reasoning, it limits the model to a single round of feature refinement before downstream tasks such as segmentation and matching.

Recent work suggests that relational reasoning benefits from multiple iterative passes. For example, PHFormer repeatedly applies intra-fragment and inter-fragment attention to progressively refine proxy-level features \cite{cui2024phformer}, while PMTR stacks multiple matching layers that first establish coarse correspondences and then refine them into precise alignments \cite{lee20243d}. These observations motivate extending Jigsaw with an additional attention pass, allowing the model to revisit and refine its feature representations.

However, unlike these approaches, which are trained from scratch, our setting starts from a pretrained Jigsaw model. Directly adding a second attention block with randomly initialized weights may disrupt the learned feature distribution during finetuning. To address this, we introduce the second attention pass using \textbf{zero-initialized gated residual connections}, inspired by the ReZero mechanism \cite{bachlechner_rezero}. This design allows the model to initially behave identically to the pretrained network, while gradually incorporating additional capacity during training.

\paragraph{Architecture.}
Let $\mathbf{f}^{(1)}$ denote the feature representation produced by the original attention block. We introduce a second self-attention layer followed by a second cross-attention layer, whose contributions are modulated by learnable scalar gates:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gated_self}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{self}}^{(2)} =
\mathbf{f}^{(1)} +
g_s \cdot \left(\text{SelfAttn}_2(\mathbf{f}^{(1)}) - \mathbf{f}^{(1)}\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gated_cross}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{cross}}^{(2)} =
\mathbf{f}_{\text{self}}^{(2)} +
g_c \cdot \left(\text{CrossAttn}_2(\mathbf{f}_{\text{self}}^{(2)}) - \mathbf{f}_{\text{self}}^{(2)}\right).
\end{equation}

Here, $g_s$ and $g_c$ are learnable scalar parameters initialized to zero. Both $\text{SelfAttn}_2$ and $\text{CrossAttn}_2$ share the same architecture as their first-pass counterparts, but use independent parameters.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
The zero-initialized gates ensure a stable integration of the new layers. At initialization, $g_s = g_c = 0$, and the second attention pass is inactive:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gated_identity}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{cross}}^{(2)} = \mathbf{f}^{(1)}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the model starts from the pretrained solution and preserves the feature distribution expected by downstream modules. During training, the gates are free to grow, allowing the model to incorporate the second attention pass only if it improves the objective.

\paragraph{Expected Benefits.}
The additional self-attention layer enables further refinement of local geometric features within each fragment, while the second cross-attention layer provides an additional opportunity for information exchange between fragments. Together, they act as a refinement stage that can improve both intra-fragment consistency and inter-fragment relationships.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The proposed modification increases computational cost and model complexity. In addition, since the contribution of the second attention pass is controlled by learned gates, it may have little effect if the optimization does not find it beneficial.

\section{Fracture Surface Segmentation}
\paragraph{Unmodified baseline component.} This module is inherited from Jigsaw without modification, but is described here because its output defines the input to both the matching module and the ICP refinement step.

Following feature extraction, the network employs a surface segmentation module to identify the overlapping fracture surfaces where pieces should connect. Jigsaw frames this as a point-wise \textbf{binary classification} task, predicting whether each point \(p\) belongs to the fracture surface or the original intact surface.

The module takes the extracted geometric feature vector \(f_p\) and passes it through two MLP layers, reducing the dimensionality to a single channel, followed by a Sigmoid activation to output a fracture confidence score \(\tilde{c}_p \in [0,1]\). The classifier is trained with a binary cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:seg_loss}
    L_{seg} =
    -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{p \in O}
    \left[
    c_p \log \tilde{c}_p
    +
    (1 - c_p) \log(1 - \tilde{c}_p)
    \right]
    \end{equation}

The ground truth label \(c_p\) is generated automatically by evaluating the physical distance from point \(p\) to its nearest neighbor on any other piece within the assembled object. A point is classified as fracture (\(c_p = 1\)) if this distance is below a predefined threshold \(\eta = 0.025\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gt_label}
c_p = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \text{Dis}(p, \text{NN}(p, P \setminus P_i)) \leq \eta \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Despite the inherent class imbalance (fracture areas are typically much smaller than intact outer surfaces), the two MLP layers provide sufficient capacity for accurate segmentation. By isolating fracture points, this module significantly reduces the search space for the subsequent matching stage, focusing it exclusively on the actual broken boundaries.

\section{Multi-Part Point Matching}
\paragraph{Unmodified baseline component.} This module is inherited from Jigsaw without modification. It is summarized here because its output (point-to-point correspondences) feeds directly into the pose recovery stage, where the proposed ICP refinement operates.

Unlike sequential pairwise methods that accumulate drift, Jigsaw evaluates all fracture points from all fragments simultaneously through a global matching module. This module computes a global matching probability matrix and uses optimal transport and assignment algorithms to predict globally consistent point-to-point connections.

\textbf{Primal-Dual Descriptors.} When a solid object breaks, the resulting pieces exhibit complementary geometry: a convex protrusion on one fragment corresponds to a concave indentation on another. Standard feature extractors are designed to find identical surfaces, which fails in this context. To address this, Jigsaw introduces the \textbf{Primal-Dual Descriptor} (Figure~\ref{fig:primal_dual_desc}).

The module takes the initial geometric embeddings of the fracture surfaces \(\hat{f}_i^c\) and projects them into two distinct representational spaces to capture both viewing directions of the same fracture. This is achieved by applying an MLP mapping, implemented as a \(1 \times 1\) convolution with a ReLU activation, followed by a normalization layer for numerical stability. The features are split into:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A Primal Descriptor \(\hat{f}_i^p\), which encodes the local convex shape of the piece.
    \item A Dual Descriptor \(\hat{f}_i^d\), which encodes the corresponding concavity as observed from the outside.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/primal-dual.png}
    \caption{The Primal-Dual Descriptor mechanism. Fracture surface features are projected into two complementary representational spaces: a primal descriptor encoding the convex geometry, and a dual descriptor encoding the corresponding concavity.}
    \label{fig:primal_dual_desc}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Global Affinity and Sinkhorn Optimal Transport.} A global affinity matrix \(M\) is computed via the scaled dot-product between primal and dual features:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:affinity}
M = \exp\!\left(\frac{\hat{F}_p^T A \hat{F}_d}{\tau}\right)
\end{equation}
where \(A\) represents learnable affinity weights and \(\tau\) is a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness of the distribution. To convert these raw affinity scores into valid probabilities, the module applies a \textbf{Sinkhorn layer}. The Sinkhorn layer transforms the affinity matrix into a doubly stochastic matrix by iteratively normalizing its rows and columns. This process enforces a soft one-to-one matching constraint: each point distributes its matching probability across all candidates, while simultaneously competing with other points to be selected. As a result, the matrix \(\tilde{X}\) approximates a permutation matrix, making it suitable for global matching.

\textbf{Training Losses.} The matching loss is a cross-entropy between predicted soft matches and a ground-truth correspondence matrix \(X_{gt}\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mat_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{mat} =
-\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{1 \le i,j \le \tilde{N}}
\left[
x_{ij}^{gt} \log \tilde{x}_{ij}
+
(1 - x_{ij}^{gt}) \log(1 - \tilde{x}_{ij})
\right]
\end{equation}
The ground truth is constructed automatically: \(x_{ij}^{gt} = 1\) if point \(j\) is the nearest neighbor of point \(i\) on a different fragment.

Even if the network correctly predicts which points correspond to one another, those matches might not translate into a physically valid rigid transformation (rotation and translation without warping). To enforce this, Jigsaw applies a \textbf{Rigidity Loss}. For each fracture point \(p\) on fragment \(i\), the matching matrix \(\tilde{X}_{ij}\) predicts a target location \(p'\) on fragment \(j\) by computing a probability-weighted average of all matchable points:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:target_location}
p' = \frac{\tilde{X}_{ij}(p) P_j}{\|\tilde{X}_{ij}(p)\|_1}
\end{equation}
Using these matched pairs, the algorithm solves the classical orthogonal Procrustes problem to find the optimal relative rotation \(\tilde{R}_{ij}\) and translation \(\tilde{t}_{ij}\) that minimize the weighted Mean Squared Error between the points. This closed-form solution is computed differentiably using Singular Value Decomposition:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:procrustes}
    R_{ij}, t_{ij} =
    \underset{R \in SO(3),\, t \in \mathbb{R}^3}{\text{argmin}}
    \sum_{p \in P_i}
    \|\tilde{X}_{ij}(p)\|_1
    \|Rp + t - p'\|_2^2
\end{equation}
The Rigidity Loss \(\mathcal{L}_{rig}\) penalizes this residual alignment error, ensuring the predicted point matches are consistent with a valid physical transformation. The total training objective combines all three losses:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total_loss}
L = \alpha L_{seg} + \beta L_{mat} + \gamma L_{rig}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Hungarian Assignment.} The Sinkhorn output \(\tilde{X}\) provides continuous soft probabilities, but physical reassembly requires discrete matches. The \textbf{Hungarian algorithm} solves the linear assignment problem in polynomial time, producing a strict binary permutation matrix \(X\) of globally optimal 1-to-1 assignments.

\section{Pose Recovery and Alignment}
\paragraph{Baseline component with proposed refinement.} The pose recovery pipeline is inherited from Jigsaw, with one proposed modification: an ICP refinement step inserted between RANSAC and Shonan averaging.

\textbf{Pairwise Pose Recovery via RANSAC.} Once the Hungarian algorithm outputs a binary correspondence matrix, the next objective is to determine the rigid transformation (rotation and translation) required to align each pair of fragments. Because the network inevitably predicts some incorrect matches, computing a transformation from all points simultaneously would yield an inaccurate alignment. RANSAC addresses this by repeatedly sampling 3 corresponding point pairs, computing a candidate transformation via SVD-based Procrustes, and evaluating how many remaining pairs align within a predefined error tolerance. The transformation supported by the largest consensus set of inliers is selected, effectively discarding outlier correspondences.

\textbf{Global Alignment via Transformation Synchronization.} While RANSAC successfully aligns fragments in isolated pairs, assembling a multi-part object from pairwise transformations alone would lead to cumulative drift. To achieve a globally consistent assembly, the framework represents the object as a factor graph where nodes are the absolute global poses of each fragment and edges are the relative pairwise transformations from RANSAC. Because a single piece may connect to multiple fragments, chaining these relative transformations often results in slight mathematical contradictions (cycle inconsistencies).

To resolve these conflicting constraints, the framework employs Shonan averaging \cite{dellaert2020shonan} (Figure~\ref{fig:shonan_avg}), a transformation synchronization algorithm. Rather than aligning pieces one by one, Shonan averaging jointly optimizes the global poses of all nodes simultaneously. It distributes and minimizes the accumulated alignment errors across all edges, finding the solution that best satisfies all local pairwise constraints at once. This results in a cohesive, drift-free final 3D reassembly.

\subsection{Proposed Improvement: ICP Refinement for Pairwise Poses}
\label{sec:icp_methodology}

The pairwise transformations estimated via RANSAC serve as the input constraints for the global alignment stage. While these transformations are robust to outliers, they are computed from sparse correspondences and therefore only approximate the true geometric alignment between fragments. To further refine these pairwise estimates before global synchronization, we introduce an Iterative Closest Point (ICP) step between RANSAC and Shonan Averaging.

\paragraph{Pipeline Integration.}
The refinement is applied to every fragment pair for which RANSAC successfully estimates a transformation (i.e., pairs with at least 3 inlier correspondences). Given such a pair, RANSAC produces an initial rigid transformation $T_{\text{ransac}}$, which is then used to initialize ICP:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:icp}
T_{\text{refined}} = \text{ICP}(P_{\text{source}}, P_{\text{target}}, T_{\text{ransac}}).
\end{equation}
The resulting refined transformations replace the RANSAC estimates as input edges in the factor graph optimized by Shonan Averaging.

\paragraph{Implementation Details.}
We use the \texttt{registration\_icp} implementation from Open3D \cite{Zhou2018open3d}. The specific configuration is as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Variant:} Point-to-point ICP (\texttt{TransformationEstimationPointToPoint}), which minimizes the sum of squared distances between closest-point pairs. We do not use point-to-plane ICP, as reliable surface normals are not available from the sparse predicted fracture points.
    \item \textbf{Input points:} Only the predicted fracture surface points (critical points) are used, not the full surface point clouds. This focuses the alignment on the geometrically informative fracture boundaries, where the complementary geometry between fragments provides the strongest alignment signal.
    \item \textbf{Maximum correspondence distance:} 0.05 (in the unit-normalized coordinate system). Point pairs farther apart than this threshold are excluded from the optimization, preventing distant outlier points from distorting the alignment.
    \item \textbf{Scaling:} Disabled (\texttt{with\_scaling=False}). Since all meshes are pre-normalized to a unit bounding box, the transformation is restricted to pure rotation and translation.
    \item \textbf{Convergence:} Open3D's default criteria are used: the algorithm terminates when the relative change in fitness falls below $10^{-6}$, or after 30 iterations, whichever comes first.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
RANSAC and ICP address complementary aspects of the alignment problem. RANSAC is designed to handle incorrect correspondences by identifying a transformation supported by a consistent subset of matches, but it relies on small random point subsets (3 per hypothesis) and does not explicitly optimize alignment over the available surface geometry. ICP, on the other hand, performs local refinement by iteratively minimizing distances between nearby points in the two fragments, exploiting the dense geometric structure of the fracture surfaces. However, as a local optimization method, ICP requires a sufficiently accurate initialization to converge to a meaningful solution.

By initializing ICP with the RANSAC estimate, the method combines robustness to outliers with local geometric refinement. RANSAC provides a stable coarse alignment, while ICP adjusts this estimate using a larger set of spatial constraints.

\paragraph{Role in the Alignment Pipeline.}
The refined transformations are incorporated into the same global synchronization framework described previously. Since Shonan Averaging operates over pairwise constraints, improving the local alignment quality results in more consistent inputs for the global optimization. This preserves the overall structure of the pipeline, while introducing an intermediate refinement step between local estimation and global alignment.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The additional ICP step increases computational cost and remains dependent on the quality of the initial transformation. Since ICP operates only on the predicted fracture points rather than the full surface, its effectiveness depends on the quality of the upstream segmentation. Furthermore, point-to-point ICP converges more slowly than point-to-plane ICP and may settle in local minima if the initial alignment is poor. In practice, however, the RANSAC initialization is sufficiently accurate for the refinement to consistently improve alignment quality (see Section~\ref{sec:incomplete}).

\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{chap:ch6}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the proposed modifications to the Jigsaw framework. We first evaluate all modifications on the full Breaking Bad dataset, then conduct a data scaling analysis by training on a reduced subset ($\sim$63\% of the everyday training patterns) to study how the proposed extensions behave under data scarcity. The proposed modifications---particularly gated double attention---provide consistent improvements when data is limited, acting as implicit regularizers that extract geometric patterns a single-pass model cannot recover from sparse examples. The data scaling analysis additionally reveals that \textbf{data quantity has a large compounding effect}, with the full dataset yielding over 10 percentage points more than the reduced subset. A further finding is that loss scheduling during finetuning has a surprisingly large effect on the gated double attention layers.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{The Breaking Bad Dataset}

All experiments are conducted on the Breaking Bad dataset \cite{sellan2022breaking}, a large-scale benchmark for 3D fracture assembly containing over one million fractured objects simulated from approximately 10,000 base models. Unlike earlier shape assembly datasets such as PartNet \cite{mo2019partnet} or AutoMate \cite{jones2021automate}, which decompose objects along semantically meaningful boundaries (effectively modeling \emph{construction}), Breaking Bad models the \emph{destruction} process---how geometric objects naturally break into fragments under physical impact.

\paragraph{Dataset construction.}
The base shapes are collected from PartNet (20 daily object categories) and Thingi10K \cite{zhou2016thingi10k} (sculptures and archaeological-style artifacts). Each mesh is rescaled to a unit bounding box and fractured using the physically-based simulation of Sell\'{a}n et al.\ \cite{sellan2022breaking}, producing 100 unique fracture patterns per base model (20 fracture modes + 80 random impacts). Patterns with fewer than 2 or more than 100 pieces are discarded.

\paragraph{Dataset subsets.}
The dataset is organized into three subsets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Everyday:} 20 categories of daily objects (BeerBottle, Bottle, Bowl, Cookie, Cup, DrinkBottle, DrinkingUtensil, Mirror, Mug, PillBottle, Plate, Ring, Spoon, Statue, Teacup, Teapot, ToyFigure, Vase, WineBottle, WineGlass), sourced from PartNet. Contains 34,075 training and 7,679 validation fracture patterns from 407 and 91 objects respectively.
    \item \textbf{Artifact:} Sculptures and archaeological-style objects from Thingi10K. Contains 3,651 fracture patterns from 40 objects. Serves as a cross-domain generalization benchmark: models are trained exclusively on everyday objects and evaluated on artifacts without any finetuning.
    \item \textbf{Other:} Remaining Thingi10K meshes (video game assets, fabrication models, etc.), which we do not use in our experiments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We adopt the standard evaluation metrics used across the 3D assembly literature \cite{lu2023jigsaw, sellan2022breaking}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chamfer Distance (CD $\downarrow$):} The average bidirectional distance between the predicted and ground-truth point clouds ($\times 10^{-3}$). For each fragment, CD measures how closely the predicted pose aligns the piece to its true position; lower values indicate tighter geometric alignment.
    \item \textbf{Part Accuracy (PA $\uparrow$):} The fraction of fragments whose Chamfer distance falls below a threshold $\tau = 0.01$. A fragment is considered correctly assembled if and only if its CD is below this threshold. PA is therefore a \emph{derived} metric that discretizes the continuous CD into a binary correct/incorrect decision per fragment, providing an intuitive overall success rate. This is the primary metric for assembly quality.
    \item \textbf{Rotation MAE ($\downarrow$):} Mean absolute error of the predicted rotation matrices, measured in degrees.
    \item \textbf{Translation MAE ($\downarrow$):} Mean absolute error of the predicted translation vectors.
\end{itemize}

All results are reported as the mean $\pm$ standard deviation over three independent evaluation runs with different random seeds to ensure statistical reliability.

\paragraph{Finetuning strategy.}
We employ full finetuning with gated residual integration of new modules. All pretrained parameters remain trainable but receive a $10\times$ lower learning rate ($10^{-4}$) than the original training, limiting how far they can drift from the pretrained solution. New modules (pair geometric encoder, second attention layers) are connected to the pretrained pathway through zero-initialized gates (Section~\ref{sec:double_attn_method}), which ensure that at initialization the network produces exactly the same output as the pretrained model. This provides the same protection against catastrophic forgetting as parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA \cite{hu2022lora}, with the added benefit that the learned gate values are directly interpretable (Section~\ref{sec:gate_introspection}).

\paragraph{Environment migration.}
The original Jigsaw codebase was developed for Python~3.8, PyTorch~1.10, and CUDA~11.3. We migrated the entire software stack to Python~3.10, PyTorch~2.7, and CUDA~12.8, resolving breaking changes across the PyTorch Geometric ecosystem, Open3D, GTSAM, and PyTorch Lightning~(1.9$\to$2.5). The migration also resolved out-of-memory failures that occurred with the original environment, likely due to improved memory management in newer PyTorch releases (e.g., refined CUDA caching allocator and reduced fragmentation). All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX~4090 (24\,GB). The migrated environment reproduces the original pretrained checkpoint's evaluation metrics, confirming that the port does not affect numerical results.

New modules receive a $10\times$ higher learning rate than pretrained parameters, since they are initialized from scratch and must learn faster to catch up with the already-converged backbone. Figure~\ref{fig:lr_schedule} shows the resulting learning rate trajectories for all parameter groups across our experiments: the baseline uses a single parameter group with cosine annealing from $10^{-4}$, while pair attention and double attention configurations use two groups---one at $10^{-4}$ for pretrained weights and one at $10^{-3}$ for new layers.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

It is important to note that our experiments focus on a restricted setting compared to the full Breaking Bad benchmark. Due to computational constraints, we fine-tune and evaluate on subsets of 2 pieces (pairwise assembly) and 2--4 pieces (limited multi-piece assembly), rather than the full 2--20 piece range used in the original Jigsaw evaluation. As a result, our absolute numbers are not directly comparable to published results that evaluate on the complete piece range. However, all comparisons between our proposed modifications and the baseline are conducted under identical conditions, ensuring internal validity.

\section{Results on the Full Dataset}
\label{sec:complete}

\subsection{Two-Piece Assembly}

As a first step, we fine-tuned the pretrained Jigsaw model exclusively on 2-piece fracture patterns from the full everyday training set using the standard loss schedule. Adding an ICP refinement step between RANSAC and Shonan averaging (Section~\ref{sec:icp_methodology}) yielded a consistent improvement of approximately 1 percentage point on both subsets. Since ICP introduces no learnable parameters, this improvement is entirely orthogonal to the other modifications. We retain ICP in all subsequent experiments.

On the full dataset, the baseline already achieves 86.4\% PA on everyday objects, leaving limited headroom for architectural improvements on in-distribution data (Table~\ref{tab:two_piece_complete}). All four modifications were evaluated: pair attention (Section~\ref{par:pair_attn_limitations}), double attention (Section~\ref{sec:double_attn_method}), and Gabriel graph filtering (Section~\ref{sec:gabriel_method}) at the best retention ratio ($r{=}0.75$; see Section~\ref{sec:gabriel_tuning} for the tuning analysis). On everyday, the baseline remains the strongest. On the \textit{artifact} subset---which serves as a cross-domain generalization benchmark---all modifications show improvements in pose metrics, with Gabriel producing the best chamfer distance ($-$10\%) and double attention the best translation error.

\section{The Effect of Loss Scheduling}
\label{sec:loss_scheduling}

During the reduced-subset experiments, one configuration was run with a non-standard loss schedule: the matching loss was delayed to epoch 9 (instead of activating immediately), and the rigidity loss was set to activate at epoch 199---effectively disabling it entirely for the 100-epoch finetuning run. This ``gentle'' schedule produced the best results of any configuration: 80.4\% part accuracy on everyday and 69.4\% on artifact, outperforming the standard-schedule double attention (78.0\% and 68.5\%).

The difference in training dynamics is visible in the loss curves. Under the standard schedule (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_standard}), all three loss terms are active early and the rigidity loss spikes at epoch 30 before decaying. Under the gentle schedule (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_gentle}), the matching loss remains near zero until epoch 9, when it jumps sharply as it activates; the rigidity loss never appears, since its activation epoch exceeds the training duration. This delayed activation allows the segmentation and matching pathways to stabilize independently before interacting.

This result reveals an important subtlety of finetuning with gated residual connections. Although the zero-initialized gates protect the pretrained features at initialization, the \textit{gradients} are not gated. Under the standard schedule, the matching loss pressures the randomly initialized layers from epoch 0, creating tension with the pretrained pathway before the new layers have calibrated. The gentle schedule avoids this by letting the new layers adapt through the softer segmentation signal first, and by removing the rigidity loss entirely, reducing the optimization burden.

\label{sec:gate_introspection}

Three patterns emerge:

\paragraph{The self-attention gate barely opens.} Across all configurations, $g_s$ remains close to zero and the second self-attention layer diverges less than 1\% from its initialization. The intra-fragment feature refinement provided by the first self-attention pass appears to be sufficient; the model finds no benefit in a second round of local reasoning.

\paragraph{The cross-attention gate opens significantly.} The gate $g_c$ learns to take negative values (up to $-0.22$), and the second cross-attention layer diverges substantially (up to 101.6\%) from its initialization. This indicates that the model is actively using the second cross-attention layer, but in a \textit{subtractive} manner: the negative gate suppresses certain inter-fragment attention patterns established by the first pass, effectively acting as a learned correction rather than an additive refinement.

\paragraph{Greater adaptation does not imply greater marginal gain.} On the full dataset, the gates open wider and the layers diverge more than on the reduced subset, yet the marginal improvement is small. This is consistent with the data scaling finding: with sufficient data, the single-pass model already captures the patterns that the second pass provided under scarcity. The two mechanisms---additional attention capacity and additional data---address the same representational bottleneck from complementary directions.

\section{Gabriel Graph: Retention Ratio Analysis}
\label{sec:gabriel_tuning}

The Gabriel filter's aggressiveness is controlled by the minimum keep ratio $r$ (Section~\ref{sec:gabriel_method}). We evaluated three configurations on the full everyday two-piece dataset to determine the optimal setting before reporting the test results in Table~\ref{tab:two_piece_complete}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/validation_loss_gabriel.png}
    \caption{Gabriel filter retention and validation performance across configurations. All models trained for 100 epochs on the full everyday two-piece dataset.}
    \label{fig:enter-label}
\end{figure}

The progression is clear: aggressive pruning ($r{=}0$) leaves max-pooling with almost no diversity and collapses performance. As more neighbors are preserved, performance improves monotonically. The 75\% configuration achieved the highest validation mat\_f1 (0.143), substantially above the baseline's $\sim$0.10, confirming that conservative filtering preserves sufficient neighborhood diversity while removing the most topologically distant neighbors.

The validation gains did not fully transfer to test (val mat\_f1 0.143 vs.\ test PA 85.9\%, compared to baseline 86.4\%). On cross-domain data (artifact), Gabriel produced the best chamfer distance ($-$10\%) and translation error ($-$5\%) of any modification (Table~\ref{tab:two_piece_complete}), suggesting that surface-faithful neighborhoods yield more geometrically precise features---the matches found are of higher quality, but fewer in total. The validation-to-test gap is likely caused by sensitivity to point density variations across objects: denser regions trigger more aggressive pruning, and the distribution of object densities differs between splits. Training from scratch with Gabriel-filtered neighborhoods would allow the encoder to learn features adapted to the reduced neighborhoods from the start.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments yield several conclusions. \textbf{Architectural modifications provide targeted improvements:} double attention yielded +2--5 points under data scarcity and consistent cross-domain gains on the artifact subset. \textbf{Data scaling has a compounding effect:} the full dataset yields over 10 points more than the reduced subset, confirming that data quantity amplifies the base architecture's capacity; on the full dataset, the baseline already captures the patterns that the extensions provided under scarcity. \textbf{ICP refinement is reliable and orthogonal:} a consistent $\sim$1 point improvement across all configurations, with no risk of overfitting. \textbf{Loss scheduling matters for gated finetuning:} a gentler activation schedule outperformed the standard one, indicating that the interaction between new gated components and pretrained representations is sensitive to optimization dynamics. \textbf{Geometric pair attention reveals an architectural compatibility constraint:} the pair encoder's effectiveness depends on the surrounding preprocessing and pose estimation context, motivating future work on a two-pass architecture that would provide the spatial signal the encoder requires. \textbf{Gabriel graph filtering improves cross-domain pose quality but reduces in-domain matching count:} surface-faithful neighborhoods produce higher-quality matches on out-of-distribution data (artifact), but the pruning reduces the total number of correspondences, slightly lowering the primary matching metric on in-domain data.

Figure~\ref{fig:val_losses} provides an overview of validation loss trajectories across all experiments. The most visible pattern is the separation between two-piece configurations (lower losses) and multi-piece configurations (higher losses), reflecting the increased difficulty of assembling more fragments. Within each group, the different architectural variants follow similar trajectories, consistent with the finding that the proposed modifications do not substantially alter the model's learning dynamics when sufficient data is available.

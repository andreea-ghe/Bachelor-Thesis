\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{chap:ch6}

This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the proposed modifications to the Jigsaw framework. To set expectations upfront: the proposed architectural modifications---pair geometric attention and gated double attention---showed initial promise when evaluated on an incomplete version of the dataset, but did not yield meaningful improvements once the full dataset was restored. The central finding of this chapter is that \textbf{data quantity was the dominant factor}: restoring the missing 37\% of training data improved part accuracy by over 10 percentage points, far exceeding any architectural change. A secondary finding is that loss scheduling during finetuning had a surprisingly large effect on the gated double attention layers.

We present the experiments chronologically, as the order in which results were obtained is itself informative. Section~\ref{sec:incomplete} reports the initial results on the incomplete dataset, where the modifications appeared beneficial. Section~\ref{sec:data_twist} explains how the data issue was discovered. Section~\ref{sec:complete} shows how the conclusions changed on the complete dataset. Sections~\ref{sec:loss_scheduling} and~\ref{sec:gate_introspection} analyze the loss scheduling effect and the learned gate behavior, respectively.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{The Breaking Bad Dataset}

All experiments are conducted on the Breaking Bad dataset \cite{sellan2022breaking}, a large-scale benchmark for 3D fracture assembly containing over one million fractured objects simulated from approximately 10,000 base models. Unlike earlier shape assembly datasets such as PartNet \cite{mo2019partnet} or AutoMate \cite{jones2021automate}, which decompose objects along semantically meaningful boundaries (effectively modeling \emph{construction}), Breaking Bad models the \emph{destruction} process---how geometric objects naturally break into fragments under physical impact.

\paragraph{Dataset construction.}
The base shapes are collected from PartNet (20 daily object categories) and Thingi10K \cite{zhou2016thingi10k} (sculptures and archaeological-style artifacts). Each mesh is treated as a solid with isotropic material properties, rescaled to fit a unit-length bounding box for scale invariance, and enclosed in a coarse tetrahedral cage (4,000 faces) generated using the Simple Nested Cages (SNC) algorithm \cite{sellan2023nested}. The first 20 \emph{fracture modes}---orthogonal, maximally different, and physically plausible break patterns---are computed from the cage using the generalized eigenvalue formulation of Sell\'{a}n et al.\ \cite{sellan2022breaking}. These modes are then transferred onto the input mesh. For each base shape, 80 random impacts are projected onto the precomputed fracture modes, producing 80 impact-dependent fracture patterns. Together with the 20 fracture modes themselves, this yields 100 unique fracture patterns per base model. Patterns producing fewer than 2 or more than 100 pieces are discarded.

\paragraph{Dataset subsets.}
The dataset is organized into three subsets:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Everyday:} 20 categories of daily objects (BeerBottle, Bottle, Bowl, Cookie, Cup, DrinkBottle, DrinkingUtensil, Mirror, Mug, PillBottle, Plate, Ring, Spoon, Statue, Teacup, Teapot, ToyFigure, Vase, WineBottle, WineGlass), sourced from PartNet. Contains 34,075 training and 7,679 validation fracture patterns from 407 and 91 objects respectively.
    \item \textbf{Artifact:} Sculptures and archaeological-style objects from Thingi10K. Contains 3,651 fracture patterns from 40 objects. Serves as a cross-domain generalization benchmark: models are trained exclusively on everyday objects and evaluated on artifacts without any finetuning.
    \item \textbf{Other:} Remaining Thingi10K meshes (video game assets, fabrication models, etc.), which we do not use in our experiments.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We adopt the standard evaluation metrics used across the 3D assembly literature \cite{lu2023jigsaw, sellan2022breaking}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chamfer Distance (CD $\downarrow$):} The average bidirectional distance between the predicted and ground-truth point clouds ($\times 10^{-3}$). For each fragment, CD measures how closely the predicted pose aligns the piece to its true position; lower values indicate tighter geometric alignment.
    \item \textbf{Part Accuracy (PA $\uparrow$):} The fraction of fragments whose Chamfer distance falls below a threshold $\tau = 0.01$. A fragment is considered correctly assembled if and only if its CD is below this threshold. PA is therefore a \emph{derived} metric that discretizes the continuous CD into a binary correct/incorrect decision per fragment, providing an intuitive overall success rate. This is the primary metric for assembly quality.
    \item \textbf{Rotation MAE ($\downarrow$):} Mean absolute error of the predicted rotation matrices, measured in degrees.
    \item \textbf{Translation MAE ($\downarrow$):} Mean absolute error of the predicted translation vectors.
\end{itemize}

All results are reported as the mean $\pm$ standard deviation over three independent evaluation runs with different random seeds to ensure statistical reliability.

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

It is important to note that our experiments focus on a restricted setting compared to the full Breaking Bad benchmark. Due to computational constraints, we fine-tune and evaluate on subsets of 2 pieces (pairwise assembly) and 2--4 pieces (limited multi-piece assembly), rather than the full 2--20 piece range used in the original Jigsaw evaluation. As a result, our absolute numbers are not directly comparable to published results that evaluate on the complete piece range. However, all comparisons between our proposed modifications and the baseline are conducted under identical conditions, ensuring internal validity.

\section{Initial Results on the Incomplete Dataset}
\label{sec:incomplete}

Our initial experiments were conducted on what we believed to be the full Breaking Bad dataset. Due to a data transfer issue, approximately 37\% of the fracture patterns were missing from the everyday subset---a fact we only discovered later (Section~\ref{sec:data_twist}). This section presents the results obtained on this incomplete dataset.

\subsection{Baseline: Finetuning for Two-Piece Assembly}

As a first step, we fine-tuned the pretrained Jigsaw model exclusively on 2-piece fracture patterns using the standard loss schedule. Table~\ref{tab:two_piece_incomplete} shows the results. This specialization produced a substantial improvement over the original multi-piece checkpoint: part accuracy increased from 57.1\% to 75.9\% on everyday and from 45.4\% to 63.8\% on artifact, indicating that the pretrained model had room for improvement when focused on a specific piece-count regime.

\subsection{ICP Refinement}

Adding an ICP refinement step between RANSAC and Shonan averaging (Section~\ref{sec:icp_methodology}) yielded a consistent improvement of approximately 1 percentage point on both subsets. Since ICP operates purely on the geometric alignment stage and introduces no learnable parameters, this improvement is entirely orthogonal to the attention-based modifications. We retain ICP in all subsequent experiments.

\subsection{Pair Geometric Attention Bias}
\label{sec:pair_attn_results}

As anticipated in Section~\ref{par:pair_attn_limitations}, the pair attention bias had minimal effect on two-piece assembly. The independent centering and rotation applied during preprocessing substantially reduces the geometric variation available to the pair encoder, limiting its ability to produce discriminative biases across fragment pairs. In the two-piece setting, with only one target fragment, the bias further reduces to a single scalar shared across all attention logits. The slight improvement on artifact (+3 points) may reflect a mild regularization effect from the additional parameters rather than a meaningful geometric signal.

\subsection{Gated Double Attention}

The gated double attention layers produced a consistent improvement across both subsets: +2.1 points on everyday (75.9\% $\rightarrow$ 78.0\%) and +4.7 points on artifact (63.8\% $\rightarrow$ 68.5\%), suggesting that the second attention pass provided useful additional representational capacity.

\section{Results on the Complete Dataset}
\label{sec:complete}

\subsection{The Data Quantity Effect}

The most notable finding was the magnitude of improvement from data alone. Finetuning the same baseline model on the complete dataset, with no architectural changes, raised part accuracy on everyday from 75.9\% to \textbf{86.4\%}---an improvement of over 10 percentage points---and reduced translation error by nearly half. This suggests that the model had been limited primarily by data quantity rather than representational capacity.

\subsection{The Architectural Modifications Lose Their Edge}

With sufficient training data, neither pair attention nor double attention produced meaningful improvements over the baseline (Table~\ref{tab:two_piece_complete}). Pair attention showed no statistically significant change, consistent with its theoretical limitation on two pieces. Notably, double attention---which had shown clear gains on the incomplete dataset---also produced \textit{no improvement} (86.0\% vs.\ 86.4\%).

The second attention pass, with its additional parameters, acted as an implicit regularizer that extracted more information from the limited data available. Once sufficient data was provided, the single-pass model captured the same patterns on its own, and the extra capacity became redundant.

\section{The Effect of Loss Scheduling}
\label{sec:loss_scheduling}

During the incomplete-dataset phase, one experiment was run with a non-standard loss schedule: the matching loss was delayed to epoch 9 (instead of activating immediately), and the rigidity loss was set to activate at epoch 199---effectively disabling it entirely for the 100-epoch finetuning run. This ``gentle'' schedule produced the best results of any configuration: 80.4\% part accuracy on everyday and 69.4\% on artifact, outperforming the standard-schedule double attention (78.0\% and 68.5\%).

The difference in training dynamics is visible in the loss curves. Under the standard schedule (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_standard}), all three loss terms are active early and the rigidity loss spikes at epoch 30 before decaying. Under the gentle schedule (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_gentle}), the matching loss remains near zero until epoch 9, when it jumps sharply as it activates; the rigidity loss never appears, since its activation epoch exceeds the training duration. This delayed activation allows the segmentation and matching pathways to stabilize independently before interacting.

This result reveals an important subtlety of finetuning with gated residual connections. Although the zero-initialized gates protect the pretrained features at initialization, the \textit{gradients} are not gated. Under the standard schedule, the matching loss pressures the randomly initialized layers from epoch 0, creating tension with the pretrained pathway before the new layers have calibrated. The gentle schedule avoids this by letting the new layers adapt through the softer segmentation signal first, and by removing the rigidity loss entirely, reducing the optimization burden.

\section{Gate Behavior Analysis}
\label{sec:gate_introspection}

To understand the behavior of the gated double attention layers beyond their downstream metrics, we examined the learned gate values and the weight divergence of the second attention layers across all configurations (Table~\ref{tab:gates}).

Three patterns emerge:

\paragraph{The self-attention gate barely opens.} Across all configurations, $g_s$ remains close to zero and the second self-attention layer diverges less than 1\% from its initialization. The intra-fragment feature refinement provided by the first self-attention pass appears to be sufficient; the model finds no benefit in a second round of local reasoning.

\paragraph{The cross-attention gate opens significantly.} The gate $g_c$ learns to take negative values (up to $-0.22$), and the second cross-attention layer diverges substantially (up to 101.6\%) from its initialization. This indicates that the model is actively using the second cross-attention layer, but in a \textit{subtractive} manner: the negative gate suppresses certain inter-fragment attention patterns established by the first pass, effectively acting as a learned correction rather than an additive refinement.

\paragraph{Greater adaptation does not imply better performance.} On the complete dataset, the gates open wider and the layers diverge more than on the incomplete dataset---yet the performance improvement is negligible. The gates are adapting, but what they learn does not meaningfully improve over the baseline established by the single-pass model with sufficient data.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments presented in this chapter yield several conclusions about the interplay between architecture, data, and optimization in 3D fracture assembly.

\paragraph{Data quantity outweighs architectural changes.} The 10-point improvement from restoring the missing 37\% of training data exceeded every architectural modification we tested. When the model architecture is already well-matched to the problem---as Jigsaw's attention-based pipeline is for geometric matching---performance scales more reliably with data than with architectural complexity.

\paragraph{Architectural modifications act as implicit regularizers in data-scarce regimes.} The double attention layers provided a measurable benefit (+2--5 points) when training data was limited, but became redundant with the full dataset. The additional capacity helped extract patterns from sparse examples that the single-pass model could not recover; once sufficient data was available, the base model learned those patterns directly.

\paragraph{ICP refinement is a reliable, orthogonal improvement.} ICP consistently improved results by approximately 1 percentage point across all configurations. As a parameter-free post-processing step, it carries no risk of overfitting or interference with training dynamics.

\paragraph{Loss scheduling matters for gated finetuning.} A gentler loss schedule outperformed the standard one, highlighting that the rate at which loss terms are activated determines whether new components integrate smoothly or disrupt pretrained representations. A systematic search over schedules remains an important direction for future work.

\paragraph{Geometric pair attention requires iterative pose refinement.}
The pair geometric encoder, adapted from GPAT \cite{Li2023GPAT}, did not produce improvements under Jigsaw's preprocessing. The mechanism is effective in GPAT because fragment poses are iteratively refined, providing progressively more informative geometric input. In Jigsaw's single-pass architecture, where fragments are independently normalized before the network sees them, the pair encoder operates under constrained input conditions that limit its ability to produce useful biases. This finding confirms that pair attention and iterative pose refinement are tightly coupled: the geometric descriptors require an evolving spatial signal that single-pass architectures do not provide. A natural future direction would be a two-pass architecture where initial pose estimates from the first pass provide meaningful coordinates for the pair encoder in a second pass.

The most visible pattern is the separation between two-piece configurations (lower losses) and multi-piece configurations (higher losses), reflecting the increased difficulty of assembling more fragments. Within each group, the different architectural variants follow similar trajectories, consistent with the finding that the proposed modifications do not substantially alter the model's learning dynamics when sufficient data is available.
